# Learning-Science Evidence Brief for Eduardo's IB 45 Plan

Context: 16-year-old DP1 student, high ability, currently failing to *start*. School 08:00–15:10 Mon–Fri, soccer Mon/Wed 16:30–18:00, sleep 22:45–06:40 (7h55m). Goals: pre-learn every topic before class, 14-day ramp before each test, protected sleep, some free time.

Each section gives: the finding, the sources, and a concrete "how to apply" line with numbers.

## 1. Activation and consistency: getting a high-ability student to START

### Finding 1a: Implementation intentions ("if-then" plans) roughly double follow-through
Gollwitzer & Sheeran's meta-analysis of 94 studies (8,000+ participants) found that specifying *when, where and how* you will act (an "if situation X, then I do Y" plan) improves goal attainment with d = 0.65, a medium-to-large effect that survives publication-bias checks. Goal intentions alone ("I'll study more") produce action in ~20–40% of cases; adding an implementation intention pushes it to ~50–70%. The mechanism is that the cue (time/place) becomes linked to the action, so starting no longer depends on deciding in the moment.
- Sources: [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology (APA record)](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2007-19538-002); [summary with d=0.65](https://goalsandprogress.com/implementation-intentions-gollwitzer-how-to/); [Gollwitzer overview PDF (NCI)](https://cancercontrol.cancer.gov/sites/default/files/2020-06/goal_intent_attain.pdf)

### Finding 1b: Fixed context cues build automaticity; a missed day does not reset the habit
Lally et al. (2010) tracked 96 people repeating a chosen behaviour daily in the same context ("after breakfast"). Automaticity rose along an asymptotic curve; median time to plateau was 66 days (range 18–254). Behaviours with strong context cues formed fastest, and missing one day reduced automaticity by less than half a point on the scale, recovering quickly. The practical lesson: same time, same place, same first action, every school day; never let one miss become two.
- Sources: [Lally et al. 2010, Eur. J. Social Psychology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674); [plain-language summary of what it does/doesn't show](https://steps.org/sources/lally-2010-habit-formation/)

### Finding 1c: Situational self-control beats willpower, and students who use it do better
Duckworth, Gendler & Gross (2016) argue that the most effective self-control is *situational*: choosing or modifying the environment before the temptation appears (their example: "the student who goes to the library without a cell phone"). Duckworth, White et al. (2016, J. Educational Psychology) ran a naturalistic study plus two field experiments with high-school and college students and found situation-modification strategies were used less often than willpower but were more effective at getting study done.
- Sources: [Duckworth, Gendler & Gross 2016, Perspectives on Psych Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1745691615623247); [Duckworth et al. 2016, "A Stitch in Time", J. Ed. Psych (manuscript)](https://psycnet.apa.org/manuscript/2016-15978-003.pdf)

### Finding 1d: Phone proximity. Ward et al. 2017 "brain drain" is real but contested; the safe prescription is still "other room"
Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017) found that the mere presence of one's own smartphone (on the desk, even face-down and silent) reduced available working-memory capacity and fluid intelligence versus phone in another room, with the largest cost for the most phone-dependent participants. Honest caveat: a pre-registered direct replication (Ruiz Pardo & Minda 2022) did not reproduce the effect, and a 2023/24 meta-analysis found only one significant pooled effect (working memory capacity), with nulls for other functions; a second 2023 meta-analysis (Böttger, Poschik & Zierer) did find negative effects of phone use/presence. The Scientific Reports 2023 study found reduced basal attentional performance with the phone present. Net: the effect is probably small-to-moderate and strongest for heavy users and on working-memory tasks, which is exactly the population and task type Eduardo is in. There is no evidence that phone-in-room *helps*, so the asymmetric bet is to remove it. Separately, Leroy's "attention residue" work shows that after switching tasks, part of your attention stays on the previous task and performance on the new one drops; Mark et al. found interruptions produce faster but more stressed work. Every phone check is a task switch.
- Sources: [Ward et al. 2017, J. Assoc. Consumer Research](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/691462); [UT Austin summary](https://news.utexas.edu/2017/06/26/the-mere-presence-of-your-smartphone-reduces-brain-power/); [Ruiz Pardo & Minda 2022 replication](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691822002323); [Meta-analysis, Media Psychology 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15213269.2023.2286647); [Böttger et al. 2023 meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10525686/); [Sci Reports 2023 basal attention](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-36256-4); [Leroy 2009 attention residue](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749597809000399); [Mark, Gudith & Klocke 2008](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)

### Finding 1e: Procrastination is mood repair; "just start for 10 minutes" and tiny first actions work
Pychyl and Sirois's research frames procrastination as avoiding the negative feeling attached to a task, not laziness. The most effective counter is getting started on a small, concrete sub-goal; once begun, the Zeigarnik/Ovsiankina effects (unfinished tasks nag to be resumed) make continuing easier than starting. Fogg's Tiny Habits model ("make it tiny" + an anchor + celebrate) works on the same logic: when the required ability is near zero, motivation stops being the bottleneck. Milkman's temptation bundling (a pleasurable thing you only get *while* doing the "should" thing) raised gym attendance 51% over 10 weeks, though it faded after routine disruptions.
- Sources: [Zeigarnik / Ovsiankina overview (Ness Labs)](https://nesslabs.com/unfinished-tasks); [Evidence-based procrastination strategies incl. Pychyl 10-minute rule](https://calmevo.com/how-to-stop-procrastinating/); [Fogg Behavior Model](https://www.behaviormodel.org/); [Milkman et al. 2014, Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784)

### How to apply for Eduardo
- Write three if-then plans on a card and keep it on the desk: "When I get home and drop my bag (≈15:40, or 18:20 on soccer days), I put the phone in the kitchen drawer and open tomorrow's first-period textbook." "When it's 20:30, I do the daily retrieval set." "When I notice I've opened Instagram during a block, I close it and write one line in the log instead."
- Fixed start times, same desk, every school day: **Mon/Wed 18:30**, **Tue/Thu/Fri 15:45**. Same first action every time (open the pre-learning sheet). Expect ~2 months before it feels automatic; count streaks in "days started", not "hours studied".
- Minimum viable session = **10 minutes** with permission to stop. In practice most sessions will run longer. A 10-minute day still counts as a streak day.
- Phone: in another room (not the desk, not the pocket) for every block. Laptop: site blocker on during blocks. Side project and gaming get a scheduled slot (e.g. 20:30–21:45 on two weekday evenings and Saturday afternoon) so they are bundled *after* the study blocks, not competing with them.
- Weekly target for the first month: **5/5 school days started**, not a minutes quota. Add minutes only after 3 consecutive weeks of 5/5.

## 2. Pre-learning before class (pretraining, pre-questions, JiTT)

### Finding
Three converging lines of evidence:
1. **Pre-training principle (Mayer et al. 2002; Mayer & Pilegard 2014 review):** learners who first learn the *names and characteristics of key concepts* before a lesson outperform controls on transfer tests across multiple experiments, because the lesson no longer has to carry both vocabulary and mechanism at once (reduced essential processing load).
2. **Prequestion / pretesting effect:** answering questions about material *before* learning it improves later retention of that material, and more recent classroom work (Pan et al. 2023, Educational Psychology Review) shows pretesting enhances learning in real classes and reduces mind-wandering during lectures. Caveat: with long (20+ min) authentic lecture videos, Toftness, Carpenter et al. 2018 found the benefit limited mainly to the prequestioned content, so the questions should target what the class will actually cover.
3. **Just-in-Time Teaching / pre-class reading:** JiTT programs in which students do a short reading and answer 2–3 questions before class raised engagement and, in one medical-school implementation, moved Board shelf-exam performance from the 59th to the 83rd percentile alongside a jump in textbook use from 1.6% to 79%.

The IES practice guide (Pashler et al. 2007, Recommendation 5a) explicitly recommends pre-questions to introduce a new topic.

- Sources: [Pre-training principle summary](https://pressbooks.pub/elearning2020/chapter/pre-training-principle/); [Mayer & Pilegard chapter listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/292884042_Principles_for_managing_essential_processing_in_multimedia_learning_Segmenting_pre-training_and_modality_principles); [Pan et al. 2023 "Pretesting Enhances Learning in the Classroom"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09805-6); [Prequestioning & pretesting review, Ed Psych Review 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5); [Toftness et al. 2018 limited effects with long videos](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211368117301596); [Just-in-Time Teaching (Wikipedia, with JiTT outcome data)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Just-in-time_teaching); [IES Practice Guide 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/20072004.pdf)

### How to apply for Eduardo
- Minimum effective pre-study per upcoming lesson: **15 minutes**: 8–10 min skim the textbook section / syllabus bullet (headings, bold terms, worked example), then 5 min writing (a) the 3–5 key terms with a one-line definition each, and (b) **3 questions** he thinks the class will answer. For math/physics, attempt one worked example cold before reading the solution (pretest).
- Do it for the **next school day's** lessons only, using the school timetable: typically 3–4 subjects → 45–60 min on a normal evening. On soccer days, cap at 2 subjects (30 min) and shift the rest to the morning block (see §6).
- In class: the goal is answering his 3 questions and noticing anything the teacher adds that he did not predict. Mark those in a different colour; they are the highest-value review items.

## 3. Retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving: schedules by subject type

### Finding
- **Retrieval practice** is one of the two "high utility" techniques in Dunlosky et al.'s 2013 monograph (with distributed practice). Adesope et al.'s 2017 meta-analysis of 217 studies found g = 0.61 for practice testing vs restudy; multiple-choice (g≈0.70) was at least as effective as free recall (g≈0.62), and mixed formats were strongest; feedback added only a little. Roediger & Karpicke (2006): after one week, students who repeatedly reread forgot ~56% of a passage, while those who repeatedly self-tested forgot only ~13%.
- **Spacing**: Cepeda et al. 2008 (1,350 participants) found the optimal gap between study and review is roughly 10–20% of the time until the test for tests weeks away, falling to ~5–10% for a test a year away. Too-short gaps waste time; too-long gaps lose the memory.
- **Successive relearning** (Rawson & Dunlosky): recall each item to a criterion of **3 correct recalls**, then relearn to 1 correct recall in **3 spaced sessions**; in classroom trials this raised exam scores and yielded ~80% retention a week after the last session. It is the practical algorithm that combines retrieval + spacing.
- **Interleaving** (mixing problem types so no two consecutive problems use the same method) had a large effect in mathematics: Rohrer et al. 2020 RCT with 787 seventh-graders, 61% vs 38% on a surprise test one month later, d = 0.83. Samani & Pan 2021 replicated in university physics: median improvements of 50% and 125% on two surprise tests with novel problems, even though students *felt* they learned less.
- Students systematically misjudge these: Kornell & Bjork found most students self-test only to check, not to learn, and rereading remains the dominant strategy.
- Sources: [Dunlosky et al. 2013 (APS)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/journals/pspi/learning-techniques.html); [Adesope et al. 2017 meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654316689306); [Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x); [Cepeda et al. 2008 "temporal ridgeline"](https://laplab.ucsd.edu/articles/Cepeda%20et%20al%202008_psychsci.pdf); [Rawson & Dunlosky successive relearning 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484); [Rohrer et al. 2020 RCT](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf); [Samani & Pan 2021, npj Science of Learning](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-021-00110-x); [Kornell & Bjork 2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03194055); [Brain-dump / free recall strategy](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/strategies/2017/free-recall)

### Schedule template (per topic taught in class)
| Touch | When | What (5–10 min per topic) |
|---|---|---|
| R0 | Same evening (day 0) | 3-min brain dump of the lesson from memory, then check notes; fix gaps |
| R1 | Day 1–2 | Retrieval: 3–5 questions / 2 problems, no notes; to 3 correct recalls |
| R2 | Day 7 | Relearn to 1 correct recall; add 1 interleaved problem from an older topic |
| R3 | Day 21 | Relearn; mixed practice set |
| R4 | Test ramp (days -14 to -1) | Folds into §4 |

This ~1/7/21 pattern respects Cepeda's 10–20% rule for tests 3–8 weeks out. Daily budget: **25–35 min** of retrieval across subjects on a normal day (4–6 topics due), done as the second block after pre-learning.

### By subject type
- **Problem-based (Math AA/AI, Physics, Chemistry):** retrieval = *doing problems cold*, never re-reading worked solutions first. From week 2 onward, every practice set is **interleaved**: take 6–8 problems from at least 3 different topics, shuffled, so the first step (which method?) has to be retrieved too. Keep a running "method card" per topic (when to use it, first move, common trap).
- **Conceptual (Economics, Biology, TOK-style reasoning):** retrieval = brain dump + explain-diagram-from-blank (draw the market/graph from memory, label, then annotate why each curve shifts). Use "why/how" elaboration questions and 2-mark/4-mark style definitions. Multiple-choice is fine and efficient here (Adesope).
- **Essay / language (English A, Portuguese, Language B, History):** retrieval = timed **10-minute outline** from a past prompt (thesis + 3 point/evidence/analysis bullets) with no notes, then compare with mark scheme; vocabulary/quotes via flashcards on the 1/7/21 schedule; one full essay per topic only during the ramp. For language B, 10 min/day of active output (speak or write) beats passive reading.

## 4. The 14-day test ramp

### Finding
- Diagnosis first: the pretesting literature above shows that taking a test *before* studying improves subsequent learning and directs attention to gaps. Metcalfe's 2017 Annual Review of Psychology synthesis concludes that errors followed by corrective feedback produce more durable learning than error-free practice, and the **hypercorrection effect** shows high-confidence errors, once corrected, are the *most* likely to be fixed permanently, provided the student actually processes the correction and an explanation, not just the right answer.
- Error logs: there is no large RCT on "mistake notebooks" per se; the support is indirect via Metcalfe's error-correction work, metacognition research (error analysis linked to problem-solving performance in college precalculus) and widespread exam-prep practice. The one documented failure mode is recording the wrong *output* rather than the *mechanism*: students with beautiful correction books still repeat mistakes when the log stops at "I got 7, answer was 9".
- Timed full past-papers are the closest match to the real exam (transfer-appropriate processing) and are the main vehicle for interleaving at the end of the ramp; Rohrer's and Samani & Pan's surprise-test results were on novel, mixed problems, which is what an IB paper is.
- Sleep in the final 48 h matters more than extra hours (§6): Gillen-O'Neel et al. 2013 found that on days a teen sacrifices sleep to study more, they have *more* academic problems the next day.
- Sources: [Metcalfe 2017, Annual Review of Psychology](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022); [Hypercorrection effect overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercorrection_(psychology)); [Learning Scientists on learning from errors](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/10/17-1); [Error analysis & metacognition, college precalculus](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373718005_Error_Analysis_and_Metacognition_of_College_Students); [Why error analysis fails (mechanism vs output)](https://edukatesg.com/2026/09/11/how-error-analysis-fails-why-recording-mistakes-does-not-automatically-repair-them/); [Pan et al. 2023 pretesting in classroom](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09805-6)

### How to apply for Eduardo: 14-day template (one subject; ~45–60 min/day on top of the normal routine)
- **Day -14 (diagnose, 60 min):** cold timed mini-paper (or 10–12 mixed questions across every topic in scope), no notes. Score by topic. Rank topics red/amber/green.
- **Days -13 to -9 (targeted, 45 min/day):** red topics only. Each session = 5 min re-explain the concept from memory, 30 min problems/outline questions, 10 min error log. Interleave one amber topic per session.
- **Days -8 to -5 (mixed practice, 45 min/day):** interleaved sets across all topics, weighted toward red/amber; start using mark schemes strictly.
- **Days -4 and -3 (full timed past paper, 90–120 min once, plus 30 min marking):** exam conditions, phone in another room, then mark against the official scheme. Every lost mark goes in the error log.
- **Day -2 (error-log pass, 45 min):** re-do *only* logged errors from scratch, cold. Anything still wrong gets a 3-line "rule" written on a card.
- **Day -1 (light, 30 min):** read the rule cards, one brain dump per topic, stop by 21:00. Bed 22:45 non-negotiable.
- **Error log format (one line per mistake):** Date | Question ref | What I wrote | Correct answer | **Why** (category: concept gap / method choice / algebra slip / misread / time) | **Rule for next time** | Confidence before (H/M/L). The "why" and "rule" columns are the whole point; high-confidence errors get a star and are reviewed first.

## 5. Weekly review ritual (Sunday, 20–30 min)

### Finding
Cal Newport's weekly plan is a single page that lays out the whole week so work can be batched and spread out, avoiding pile-ups and late nights; daily time-block plans then give every minute a job and get *revised*, not abandoned, when derailed. His "fixed-schedule productivity" (decide the hours first, fit work inside) matches Eduardo's protected sleep and free time. David Allen's GTD Weekly Review has three phases: **Get Clear** (empty inboxes, capture loose ends), **Get Current** (review projects, calendar past 2 weeks and next 2 weeks, next actions), **Get Creative** (bigger-picture ideas). Allen's full version takes 1–3 hours; a student version compresses to under 30 minutes.
- Sources: [Newport, "Plan Your Week in Advance"](https://calnewport.com/deep-habits-plan-your-week-in-advance/); [Newport, planning every minute](https://calnewport.com/deep-habits-the-importance-of-planning-every-minute-of-your-work-day/); [Time-Block Planner](https://www.timeblockplanner.com/); [GTD Weekly Review official PDF](https://gettingthingsdone.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/Weekly_review.pdf); [Todoist weekly review guide](https://www.todoist.com/productivity-methods/weekly-review)

### How to apply for Eduardo: Sunday 18:00–18:30 checklist
1. **Clear (5 min):** empty backpack, school portal, and any notes into one list. Write down every loose worry.
2. **Score last week (5 min):** days started / 5; retrieval touches done / due; error-log entries; a one-line "what broke the routine" note (this is the data the plan is tuned on).
3. **Current (10 min):** open the school timetable and the assessment calendar. For each of the next 5 school days, list the lessons → pre-learning items (§2) and the retrieval touches due (§3). Check: is any test within 14 days? If yes, start/continue the ramp (§4) and cut pre-learning to the minimum 15-min version for the ramp subject.
4. **Block the week (5 min):** on one page, put in the fixed items (school, soccer, sleep), then the study blocks (Mon/Wed 18:30–20:00; Tue/Thu/Fri 15:45–17:30 plus 20:00–20:45), then the free/side-project/gaming slots. If free time isn't written down it will be taken from sleep.
5. **Creative (3 min):** one thing to try next week (a single change), and a look at the IB 45 / Stanford horizon to remember why.
6. **Nightly (3 min, 21:45):** tick the day, write tomorrow's first action on the card, phone out of the bedroom.

## 6. Sleep and adolescent cognition

### Finding
- Adolescents need **8–10 h** per night (AASM/AAP consensus); sleep restriction measurably impairs memory encoding, consolidation and processing speed, and even modest increases in sleep improve cognitive function and grades.
- Sleep after learning actively consolidates declarative memory in adolescents; being deprived of sleep after study leaves new material less integrated into long-term memory (Potkin & Bunney 2012, PLoS ONE, early adolescents).
- Gillen-O'Neel, Huynh & Fuligni 2013 (535 students, daily diaries in grades 9, 10 and 12): regardless of how much a student usually studies, on days they study more *at the expense of sleep* they report more trouble understanding class material and more trouble on tests/assignments the next day; by grade 12 this trade-off was daily. Cramming into the night is a net-negative strategy.
- Chronotype: adolescence shifts most teens toward eveningness. Evening-type teens learned more and were more motivated in afternoon science classes; morning-type students outperform on morning tests. Working memory tends to be better in the evening across chronotypes. Sleep inertia (grogginess) lasts ~15–30 min for most people, with complex tasks impaired longest; performance recovers most of the way by ~20 min but full recovery can take up to an hour.
- Sources: [Potkin & Bunney 2012, sleep improves memory in early adolescence (PLoS ONE)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3413705/); [Adolescent sleep & school performance review (Sleep Medicine 2019)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2468867319301786); [IBE-UNESCO science-of-learning brief on adolescent sleep](https://solportal.ibe-unesco.org/articles/sleep-learning-and-school-start-times-in-adolescence/); [Gillen-O'Neel et al. 2013, Child Development](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2012.01834.x); [UCLA press summary](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/release/cramming-for-a-test-dont-do-it-say-ucla-researchers); [Evening types learn more in the afternoon (Learning & Individual Differences)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608016302138); [Chronotype, time of day and intelligence tests in school](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9862939/); [Chronotype & grade retention, npj Science of Learning 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-023-00171-0); [Sleep inertia: current insights](https://www.dovepress.com/sleep-inertia-current-insights-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-NSS)

### How to apply for Eduardo
- 22:45–06:40 gives 7h55m, already at the *bottom* of the recommended range. Treat 22:45 as a hard stop: **no study block may start after 21:45**, and screens (phone, side project) end at 22:15. Never trade sleep for study, especially the night before a test; the evidence says it lowers next-day performance.
- **Evening beats early morning** for his age and for memory-heavy work. Keep the main pre-learning + retrieval blocks in the afternoon/evening (15:45–17:30 or 18:30–20:00, plus 20:00–20:45).
- The 06:40 wake gives at most ~50 minutes before leaving; the first 20–30 are sleep-inertia time. Use the morning only for a **10-minute low-load task**: rereading the 3 pre-learning questions for the day's first lessons, or a flashcard pass, not new material or hard problems. No alarm earlier than 06:40.
- Weekend: keep wake time within 1 hour of 06:40 (up to 07:40) to avoid the "social jet lag" that makes Monday mornings worse; Saturday morning is a good slot for the timed past paper during a ramp.

## One-page summary of the numbers
- Start cue: same time, same desk, phone in another room; Mon/Wed 18:30, Tue/Thu/Fri 15:45. Minimum session 10 min. Target 5/5 started days before adding minutes. Expect ~2 months to automaticity.
- Pre-learning: 15 min per next-day lesson (skim + 3–5 terms + 3 questions), 45–60 min on a normal evening, 30 min on soccer days.
- Retrieval: 25–35 min/day; per topic at day 0, 1–2, 7, 21; interleave problem sets from week 2; recall to 3 correct then relearn 3 times.
- Ramp: 14 days, 45–60 min/day; diagnose day -14, target -13 to -9, mixed -8 to -5, full timed paper -4/-3, error-only pass -2, light -1.
- Weekly review: Sunday 18:00–18:30, six steps; nightly 3-min close at 21:45.
- Sleep: 22:45–06:40 fixed; last study block ends 21:45; screens off 22:15; morning = 10 min light review only; weekend wake ≤07:40.
