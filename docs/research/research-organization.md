# Organization & Strategy Research for an IB45 System
*Scope: organization and strategy only (content is handled by per-subject agents). Student: DP1, Math AA HL / Physics HL / Econ HL / Chem SL / English A SL / Portuguese A SL. Tests announced ~14 days ahead. Researched 2026-09-22.*

## 1. How 45-scorers actually organize

**Source caveat.** Reddit could not be fetched directly (r/IBO is blocked to the fetcher), so the primary accounts below come from named graduates published on tutoring/IB-community sites: Rachel (45, [U2 Tuition](https://www.u2tuition.com/resources/perfect-ib-score)), Katie Tracy (45, IBlieve co-founder, [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SoRGNJU13i4), [newsletter](https://iblieve.substack.com/p/the-secrets-to-score-a-perfect-45)), Kutay Bozkurt (41, 7/7/6 in AA HL/Physics HL, [first-person results post](https://mkutay.substack.com/p/the-results-of-my-lifes-last-two)), plus synthesized profiles from [RevisionDojo "Day in the life of a 45"](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/a-day-in-the-life-of-a-45-point-ib-graduate), [RevisionDojo "Is 45 possible"](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/is-it-actually-possible-to-get-a-45-on-the-ib-exam-reality-check-and-success-plan), [KIS Academics](https://kisacademics.com/blog/inside-an-ib-45-the-anatomy-of-a-perfect-score/), and [IBlieve grad interviews](https://iblieve.org/ib-grad-interview-regreys-and-advice-for-current-students/). About 1-2% of candidates score 45 in a typical session (RevisionDojo).

### Patterns that recur across many accounts (high confidence)

1. **Notes are indexed to the syllabus, not to the lesson.** Rachel: "I would write my notes for that topic underneath that [syllabus] heading and repeat this for each heading, just to make sure I covered everything within the syllabus." The RevisionDojo profile says the same thing differently: "write notes like an examiner: short, structured, and built for later recall." Coverage is checked against the guide's list of learning outcomes, so gaps are visible.
2. **Notes are subject-typed.** Every account splits subjects into (a) practice-driven (Math, Physics, Chem: past papers + a mistake record, minimal notes) and (b) memory/essay-driven (English, languages, humanities: essay plans, quote banks, vocabulary decks). Rachel for humanities: "I didn't write any notes for my IB revision that weren't incorporated into an essay plan." Kutay on maths: "I didn't study that hard for maths" beyond homework and past-paper mistake review, yet scored 95/100.
3. **The core loop is question -> markscheme -> fix -> retest, and it is short.** RevisionDojo describes the 45-scorer loop as learn -> test -> markscheme-aligned feedback -> fix -> retest "within 48 hours," and the daily block as 45-60 min targeted questions + 15 min fixing mistakes immediately. Kutay "documented wrong answers with markscheme solutions to memorize expected responses" and did ~60 hours of Physics past papers.
4. **A visible, hour-granular time plan.** Rachel used a colour-coded Excel grid, one cell per hour, subject + paper per cell, plus a weekly planner naming the specific topics. This is the single most concrete "organization artifact" in the primary accounts.
5. **Fewer hours, more feedback; predictability over heroics.** "A 45-point graduate tends to do the opposite: fewer hours, more feedback" and "they became predictable. They studied in ways that produced feedback, and they fixed what the feedback showed" (RevisionDojo). Typical baselines cited: [8-15 h/week outside class as sustainable](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/how-many-hours-should-ib-students-study), 15-25 h/week in intensive periods. No account claims 30+ h/week year-round.
6. **One timed paper per week, then pattern analysis of the errors** (RevisionDojo weekend routine; Kutay's method; RevisionDojo weekly checklist "one timed session weekly, mistake log with reattempts").
7. **IA/EE handled as scheduled subjects, started in DP1/summer.** "A 45-pointer doesn't ignore IAs, EE, or TOK. They just stop it from leaking into everything." Katie Tracy started her EE in the summer between DP1 and DP2, which freed Nov-Dec of DP2 for content revision. RevisionDojo's [advice compilation](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/what-advice-can-you-give-to-a-new-ib-student-21-success-tips-from-graduates-and-experts): "IAs started in Grade 11 are almost always stronger than those started in Grade 12." Rachel: EE needs "at least your fifth or sixth draft"; update ManageBac/CAS immediately "as the longer you leave it the more tedious it will get."
8. **Regrets are about stress and starting late, not about hours.** IBlieve interviewee: regretted "worrying too much... it took a toll on mental health," wished they had taken it "day by day" and known their learning style earlier. The generic graduate line "I wish I started earlier" is glossed by RevisionDojo as "they wish they had started *messier*" (first drafts allowed to be bad). Kutay's regret: 125+ hours on an EE that scored C, i.e. effort without early criterion-checking.

### One-off advice (lower confidence, use selectively)
- Morning "too small to fail" 20-minute routine (10 min flashcards + 10 min clarifying one concept) and sleep before 23:00 (RevisionDojo profile).
- Whole textbook read in one winter break (Kutay) - works for him, not generalizable.
- Duolingo daily for language B (Rachel) - not relevant to a Language A.
- KIS Academics' "hardest subject in the morning, IA/EE in the afternoon, review in the evening" split - sensible but not grounded in a 45-scorer's account.

**"How far ahead of class were they?"** No account claims to run far ahead of the class. The recurring pattern is being *fully consolidated up to the current lesson* (nothing unreviewed older than a week) rather than pre-learning; the exceptions are holiday sprints (Kutay's textbook read) used to pull ahead before exam terms.

## 2. Cal Newport's *How to Become a Straight-A Student*, adapted to IB

Sources: [Houmann's detailed notes](https://bagerbach.com/books/how-to-become-a-straight-a-student/), [Sta Ines summary](https://medium.com/@luigi.sta.ines/how-to-become-a-straight-a-student-by-cal-newport-22729f535a41), [e-student Q/E/C guide](https://e-student.org/qec-note-taking-method/), [Newport's note-taking index](https://calnewport.com/monday-master-class-the-study-hacks-guide-to-note-taking/).

**A. "Study hard, study fast" (anti-pseudo-work).** Work accomplished = time spent x intensity of focus. Pseudo-work is long, low-intensity, distracted studying. Rules: max ~50-60 min sprints then a real break; isolated location, no phone; "work on something every day - a marathon, not sprints grouped together"; study in daylight hours where possible.
*IB adaptation:* the 16:20-18:30 block becomes two 50-minute sprints, each with one named deliverable (e.g. "AA HL: 8 past-paper questions on binomial + error log entries").

**B. Capture-then-schedule daily ritual (5 minutes).**
1. *Capture*: anything that lands during the day (a teacher announces a test, a due date shifts, a task appears) goes on one running capture list the moment it appears. Never held in the head.
2. *Transfer* (next morning, or that evening): move every captured item into the calendar/plan with a realistic duration; overflow is pushed to a specific later date, not left floating.
3. *Plan the day*: write today's blocks with concrete outputs.
Justification beyond Newport: [Masicampo & Baumeister (2011)](https://mindwtr.app/gtd-psychology) found that making a concrete when/how plan for an unfinished task removes the intrusive-thought cost as effectively as completing it. Capture + schedule is therefore an attention-protection device, not just tidiness.

**C. Q/E/C notes (non-technical: English A, Portuguese A, Econ theory/evaluation, TOK).** For each reading or lesson: the *Question* the material answers; the *Evidence* offered; the *Conclusion* drawn. Target ~1 page per chapter/article. Newport argues arguments, not facts, are what essays reward; Q/E/C forces argument-level notes.
*IB adaptation:* one Q/E/C block per Econ real-world example or per literary text theme, so each block is already an evaluation paragraph or PEEL paragraph in embryo.

**D. Technical courses: sample problems + "technical explanation questions".** In class, record the *problem statement, the steps, and the key insight at each step*, not the lecture prose. For revision, build a *mega problem set*: all assigned problems + in-class examples + past-paper questions + a set of *technical explanation questions* ("Explain why the impulse-momentum form is preferred here"; "State when the Maclaurin approach fails"), answered from memory without notes. This is Newport's version of self-explanation, which physics learning research links to high outcomes (students who "spontaneously explained each step of the solution" learned most, see [error-log write-up](https://note.com/kaikei_yuuki_ota/n/ncc6d822cbbaa?hl=en)).
*IB adaptation:* each Math AA HL / Physics HL / Chem SL topic gets (i) a problem set file and (ii) a 10-15 item "explain" list; a topic is "test-ready" when both are done cold.

**E. Quiz-and-recall.** Prepare, then lecture the material aloud from memory (Feynman); anything you cannot explain goes back on the list. Spread over days, not one session.

**F. The three-phase essay process (Newport gives phases, not a strict "3 days"; below is the IB-sized version).**
- *Day 1 - Thesis hunt*: pick topic, follow reference chains general -> specific, get the question/thesis approved by the teacher.
- *Day 2 - Skeleton*: topic-level outline with quotes/evidence pasted under each point; then draft following the outline (Newport: 3-5 pages weekday, 5-8 weekend).
- *Day 3 - Three editing passes*: (1) argument-adjustment pass on screen; (2) out-loud pass on paper; (3) sanity pass.
*IB adaptation:* use this for every English/Portuguese essay, Econ Paper 1 practice, TOK, and each IA/EE draft cycle; the skeleton step is where a teacher can give the cheapest, highest-value feedback.

## 3. Always-ready strategy for 2-week-notice tests

**The design goal:** every subject sits within 14 days of "test-ready," so the announcement triggers a *finishing sprint*, not a *learning sprint*.

**Evidence base.**
- *Spacing:* Cepeda et al. (2008), 1,350 learners: optimal review gap is roughly 10-20% of the retention interval, so for a test ~2 weeks out the review gap should be a few days; for end-of-year exams months out, gaps of weeks are right ([summary](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CVRWP2008.html)). A [2025 meta-analysis on spacing and retrieval in mathematics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10035-1) and the [distributed-practice classroom meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12189222/) both find positive effects; a [German school maths study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211949319300183) found strong 6-week retention gains, largest for medium-performing students.
- *Interleaving/cumulative problem sets:* Rohrer et al. (2020), preregistered RCT, 787 students, 54 classes: interleaved (mixed old + new) assignments beat blocked on an unannounced test, 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83 ([paper](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED611830.pdf)). "More than a dozen" RCTs point the same way. Mechanism: the student must *choose the strategy*, which is exactly what an unannounced or mixed-topic test demands.
- *Cumulative exams:* [Lawrence et al. 2021 (PLOS One)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0250143), intro biology: cumulative midterms gave no short-term benefit; at 5 months, low-reasoning students retained ~15% more with cumulative, *high-reasoning students ~15% more with non-cumulative* in a course already heavy on retrieval. Implication for a top student: cumulative *quizzing* is the lever, not making every test cumulative; do not over-engineer it.
- *Quiz frequency:* [Shenyang high-school RCT (2026)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691826011133), 100 students, 12 weeks, maths/science/history: medium-frequency (bi-weekly) quizzing beat both low and high frequency on recall, application and retention; daily quizzing showed diminishing returns and ate consolidation time.
- *Successive relearning:* Rawson & Dunlosky: retrieve-with-feedback until correct, then relearn in later sessions to the criterion again; classroom studies (incl. [a high-stakes biopsychology exam](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/acp.3699)) show gains that appear only after several sessions ([overview](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09637214221100484)).

**What the system looks like (concrete):**
1. **Rolling retrieval, per subject, 3 tiers.** Tier A (this week's topics): daily 10-min recall (flashcards or 2-3 problems). Tier B (last 2-6 weeks): every 3-4 days. Tier C (older): fortnightly, folded into the mixed set below.
2. **Weekly mixed problem set (Sat/Sun, 60-90 min, one science/math subject rotating):** ~50% current unit, ~50% drawn from all earlier units, problems shuffled (Rohrer's interleaving). This single artifact *is* the "cumulative quizzing" evidence applied.
3. **Fortnightly mini-diagnostic per subject** (20-30 min, timed, markscheme-marked): a small sample across all topics taught so far. Its output is a per-topic score that updates the "readiness" field (Section 4). This matches the bi-weekly optimum in the Shenyang trial.
4. **Definition of "test-ready" (per topic):** last mini-diagnostic >= 85% *and* every error-log item from that topic has a successful reattempt *and* the "explain" list (Section 2D) can be answered cold.
5. **When a test is announced:** day 0 capture -> that evening a 14-day sprint plan: days 1-4 close red topics; days 5-10 two full past-paper sessions with markscheme; days 11-13 error-log reattempts and explain-list; day 14 light review, early sleep. The sprint only *adds* the subject-specific past papers; the rolling system keeps running for other subjects.

## 4. Assessment-tracking & capture: the data model

**What good student systems track** (synthesis of [Notion assignment trackers](https://www.notion.com/templates/assignment-and-exam-tracker-for-students), [IB planners](https://eclassopedia.com/wp-content/files/Notion%20Templates%20for%20IB.pdf), [error-log practice from JEE/AP prep](https://sparkl.me/blog/ap/error-logs-that-actually-improve-scores-turn-every-mistake-into-ap-gold/), the metacognition literature on [exam wrappers and calibration](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4451238/), and self-regulated-learning research on [learning diaries](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00422/full)). Most commercial templates track *deadlines and status only*; they omit the three things that actually move grades: an error log, per-topic readiness, and teacher-feedback history.

**Tool comparison (brief).** Paper: fastest capture, no queries, no rollups; fails as the source of readiness/error data. Calendar only: great for dates, useless for topics/errors. Notion/database: supports rollups (coverage %, readiness by subject), relations (assessment -> topics -> errors), but capture friction is higher, so it needs a fast inbox. Recommended: one database system + a *capture inbox* that is reachable in under 10 seconds (a pinned quick-add note or a single "Inbox" page), processed nightly.

**The 60-second capture rule.** When a teacher says "test in two weeks on X," within 60 seconds write *one line* in the inbox: `Subject | Test | date | topics as heard | source=teacher`. Nothing else. That evening the line is promoted to an Assessment record and the sprint plan is generated. (Newport's capture principle + Masicampo & Baumeister: a captured, scheduled item stops costing attention.)

**Data model**

*Subject* (6 rows): `name`, `level (HL/SL)`, `teacher`, `paper structure (P1/P2/P3 + weights)`, `IA type + internal deadlines`, `current grade (teacher-reported)`, `predicted grade (own estimate)`, `syllabus coverage % (rollup from Topics: taught)`, `readiness % (rollup: topics test-ready)`, `open error count (rollup)`, `last mini-diagnostic date + score`, `weekly hours target`, `notes system type (practice / Q-E-C)`.

*Topic* (one row per guide sub-topic, ~30-60 per subject): `subject`, `guide code (e.g. AA HL 1.9)`, `name`, `status (not taught / taught / consolidated / test-ready)`, `date taught`, `notes done (y/n)`, `problem set done (y/n)`, `explain-list done (y/n)`, `last retrieval date`, `next retrieval due` (computed from tier), `last diagnostic score`, `confidence (1-5, self-rated)`, `calibration gap = confidence vs last score` (students are systematically overconfident in maths, see [calibration research](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4451238/)), `open errors (relation)`.

*Assessment* (tests, essays, IAs, EE milestones, mocks): `subject`, `type (class test / timed essay / IA draft / mock / EE milestone / TOK)`, `announced date`, `due/sit date`, `days notice`, `topics covered (relation)`, `format (paper, duration, calculator)`, `source of info (teacher / ManageBac / peer)`, `sprint plan link`, `predicted score (before)`, `actual score`, `grade boundary equivalent`, `teacher feedback (verbatim)`, `feedback actions (checklist)`, `exam wrapper: what worked / what failed / what changes` (exam wrappers are the standard metacognitive tool), `status`.

*Error* (the error log, the highest-value table): `date`, `subject`, `topic (relation)`, `source (homework / past paper / test / diagnostic)`, `question ref`, `error type (concept / method / algebra-sign / misread / time / command term)`, `what I did`, `what the markscheme wanted`, `fix (one sentence)`, `reattempt 1 date + result`, `reattempt 2 date + result`, `closed (y/n)`. Rule from the JEE/AP practice: an error closes only after two spaced correct reattempts; *falling weekly error counts* are the health metric.

*IA / EE project*: `subject`, `milestones (topic approved, data/plan, draft 1..n, final)`, `each milestone: internal date, school date, status`, `criterion checklist with self-score per criterion`, `teacher feedback per draft`, `hours logged`.

*Daily log* (feeds the nightly report): `date`, `blocks planned`, `blocks completed`, `retrieval done per subject (y/n)`, `errors added / closed`, `energy 1-5`, `sleep time`, `one-line note`.

## 5. Consistency & predicted grades

**How predictions are formed.** The IB defines the predicted grade as the teacher's prediction "based on all the evidence of the candidate's work and the teacher's knowledge of IB standards," and warns schools against systematic over/under-prediction; there is no IB formula, and schools are monitored ([Lanterna](https://lanterna.com/resources/ib-predicted-grades-demystified-your-ultimate-guide), [IB coordinator guidance](https://ibcoordinator.com/predicted-grades/)). The coordinator guidance is explicit that teachers are asked to weigh "overall knowledge of their students... as well as academic ability this can also include motivation, work rate, attitude to learning and self-management," and that "there is room for students to make further progress after the mock exams."

**Weighting in practice** ([RevisionDojo process article](https://www.revisiondojo.com/blog/how-do-schools-predict-ib-scores-inside-the-ib-predicted-grade-process), [Lanterna](https://lanterna.com/resources/ib-predicted-grades-demystified-your-ultimate-guide), [THE counsellor piece](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/counsellor/counselling/how-manage-ib-predicted-grades)): five buckets - mocks (heaviest: timed, markscheme-marked), IA drafts (externally moderated so treated as credible anchors), class tests/quizzes (read for *trajectory*: "one brilliant test doesn't outweigh six mediocre ones"), coursework habits (deadlines, response to feedback: they change how *borderline* evidence is read), and departmental standardisation against grade descriptors and past cohorts.

**The teacher-training counterview.** An IB workshop leader's [webinar summary](https://www.kpburgess.com/post/reporting-and-predicting-grades-in-ib) tells teachers to *not* let "effort, attitude or linguistic fluency" drive the grade, to ignore single anomalies (good or bad), and to predict from the grade descriptors using "can-do" statements the student *consistently* demonstrates across many data points. Combined with the coordinator guidance, the honest reading is: **consistency is the currency**. A student whose evidence is stable-or-rising across many small assessments is easy to predict high; a spiky student forces the teacher toward the median.

**Does visible organization move predictions?** Evidence is indirect. Halo-effect experiments show graders' marks can be shifted by unrelated cues ([Educational Psychology 2023 experiment](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01443410.2023.2194593)); classroom halo research shows teachers tend to assume the well-behaved student is also bright and motivated ([Simply Psychology review](https://www.simplypsychology.org/halo-effect.html)). The IB-specific sources say effort and reliability affect how teachers *interpret uncertain evidence* rather than override it. Practical implication: visible organization pays off *at the margin* (a 6/7 borderline), and mostly through its side-effects: deadlines met, feedback acted on, drafts improved between versions - which the kpburgess method explicitly counts as evidence.

**DP1 exams.** International schools commonly run formal end-of-DP1 exams (some in March, most in May/June), primarily to rehearse exam conditions and secondarily as summative breadth checks ([ISB assessment page](https://sites.google.com/isbusan.org/isbdp/assessment)). Lanterna's DP1 parent guide says end-of-DP1 exams "heavily influence" predictions and that predictions are typically first formed "toward the end of DP1 or early in DP2" ([guide](https://lanterna.com/resources/parent-guide-track-dp1-progress-grades)). Several schools release predictions at set points - May of DP1, then Oct/Dec/April of DP2 ([Tutopiya mocks guide](https://www.tutopiya.com/blog/ib-dp/ib-dp-mocks-guide/)), and the THE article describes a school adding an end-of-grade-11 release specifically so students can build university lists. So for early applications (Stanford REA in Nov of DP2) the DP1 June exams plus the first DP2 term are effectively *the* evidence; there is little room to recover afterward.

## 6. Energy & load management (3 HL quant-heavy subjects)

**Time-of-day.** Adolescents are, on average, later chronotypes; a large analysis found evening testing associated with higher scores in native language, English, maths and reasoning, with the biggest gains for adolescents and young adults, and a post-lunch dip ([One Health Trust summary](https://onehealthtrust.org/publications/peer-reviewed-articles/adolescents-cognitive-performance-peaks-in-the-evening-isolating-time-of-day-effects/)); synchrony with chronotype predicts performance in adolescents ([Goldstein et al. 2007](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191886906002947)). But late-type students as a group have *lower* grades, shorter sleep and lower self-regulation ([university-wide chronotyping study](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.08.04.455177.full.pdf)), so the actionable advice is "use the late afternoon/early evening, protect sleep," not "study late."

**Cognitive fatigue.** Mental fatigue accumulates with prolonged effortful work and degrades decision quality ([conceptual analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6119549/)); Ericsson's expert violinists did their hardest practice "in the late morning or early afternoon, when they were still fairly fresh" ([summary](https://jasonhaaheim.com/the-diminishing-returns-of-practicing-on-empty/)). Newport's rule (max ~1 h sprints, real breaks) is the practical version.

**Memory-type timing.** In 16-17-year-olds, declarative material (word pairs) learned at 15:00 was retained better at 24 h than the same material learned at 21:00, whereas procedural skill gained more from evening learning before sleep ([Holz et al. 2012, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0040963)). Reading: put *new declarative learning* (Econ definitions, Chem facts, literature quotes) in the afternoon block and *procedural rehearsal* (worked problems you already understand, timed drills) closer to the end of the evening.

**Placement rule for this student (16:20 start after school):**
- 16:20-16:35 buffer: snack, capture-inbox processing, plan the two sprints.
- 16:35-18:30 (two sprints, fresh): the day's hardest *new* thing - usually the HL problem set (AA HL / Physics HL) or new Econ/Chem content. This is the "hardest first" window; the evidence for it is fatigue accumulation, not a morning-person assumption.
- Dinner + genuine break (the RevisionDojo 45-profile insists on this; encoding benefits from rest).
- 20:00-21:00: reading/Q-E-C notes, essay skeletons, IA writing (lower executive load than novel problem-solving).
- 21:00-21:30: rolling retrieval (Tier A/B flashcards, 2-3 mixed problems), which is procedural and benefits from sleep proximity.
- **Hard stop 21:30-22:00, lights out by ~23:00.** The case: sleep-deprived teens show worse attention and grades ([IvyWise summary of the sleep literature](https://www.ivywise.com/blog/staying-up-late-to-study-hurts-students-more-than-it-helps/)); sleep deprivation after learning impairs consolidation ([meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8893218/)); Newport's fixed-schedule productivity/shutdown ritual makes leisure guilt-free and forces prioritisation during the day ([Newport](https://calnewport.com/drastically-reduce-stress-with-a-work-shutdown-ritual/)). The hard stop is what makes the plan honest: if work does not fit, the *plan* is wrong, not the student.

## 7. Motivation maintenance over 20 months

**Process over outcome visualization.** [Pham & Taylor (1999)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167299025002010): freshmen who mentally simulated the *process* of studying for 5-7 days before a midterm studied more and scored higher; those who visualized the *outcome* (the grade) did not; the effect ran through better planning and lower anxiety. Design consequence: "IB 45 / Stanford" belongs on the wall as an identity anchor, but the nightly and morning rituals should rehearse *tomorrow's blocks*, not the final score.

**Identity-based motivation.** Oyserman's theory: people act in identity-congruent ways, and *how difficulty is interpreted* is decisive - "difficulty-as-importance" vs "difficulty-as-impossibility" ([2024 review](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/spc3.70028)). Her School-to-Jobs intervention (timelines from current to future self, obstacles named, struggle normalised) raised grades, homework time and attendance and cut depressive symptoms ([Stanford SPARQ summary](https://sparq.stanford.edu/solutions/raise-grades-mapping-your-future-self)). The line "the most organized student in the world" is useful precisely because it is an *identity* whose daily evidence is cheap to produce (a processed inbox, a closed error), and because it reframes hard problem sets as "what someone like me does" rather than as a threat.

**Streaks and the what-the-hell effect.** Polivy & Herman's abstinence-violation effect: once a rule is broken, people interpret the slip as total failure and abandon the regime (dieters who thought they had blown their limit ate >50% more, see [summary](https://pressurepoint.substack.com/p/your-habit-tracker-is-killing-your)). Streak counters that reset to zero encode exactly this framing. (Widely quoted "63% more likely to quit after one missed day, JPSP 2020" could not be traced to a verifiable paper and should not be cited.) Conversely, [Lally et al. 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674) (96 participants, 12 weeks): "missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process"; automaticity dipped less than half a point and recovered. Habit formation took a median 66 days (range 18-254), so the first ~2-3 months of DP1 are the habit-building window and the metric should be *rate*, not *streak*.

**Self-compassion beats self-criticism for improvement.** [Breines & Chen (2012)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0146167212445599), four experiments: a self-compassionate framing after failure led to *more* study time on a subsequent hard test and stronger motivation to fix weaknesses than self-esteem or control framings. Process praise after setbacks sustains motivation where person praise ("you're smart") collapses it ([Haimovitz & Corpus 2011](https://www.reed.edu/psychology/motivation/assets/downloads/Haimovitz_Corpus_2011.pdf); [Dweck](https://teaching.temple.edu/sites/teaching/files/resource/pdf/Dweck-Perils%20&%20Promises%20of%20Praise.pdf)).

**Nightly report design (so it sustains rather than punishes).**
1. Report *rates and counts*, never consecutive-day streaks: "retrieval done 5/6 subjects," "errors closed 3, added 2," "blocks completed 3/4."
2. Show a *7-day rolling* view, so one bad day is visibly one-seventh, not a reset.
3. Every entry has a process question ("what did the plan get wrong?") and one concrete next action; no "grade yourself" field.
4. Language is process-praise: "you reattempted two errors" not "good job, genius"; failure entries auto-prompt a Breines-style line ("everyone at this level misses days; what is tomorrow's first block?").
5. End the report with tomorrow's first sprint written out (Pham & Taylor process simulation, Newport's planning ritual, Masicampo's closed loops) and a "shutdown complete" mark.
6. Keep one identity metric visible weekly: "days the inbox was processed" - the cheapest proof of "the most organized student," achievable even on sick days.

## Design implications (rules for the system)

1. **One inbox, 60-second capture.** Any announcement (test, deadline change, feedback) is written as one line in a single place within a minute; it is processed into the database every evening. Nothing lives in memory.
2. **Notes are indexed by guide sub-topic, not by date**, and each subject is typed: practice-driven (AA HL, Physics HL, Chem SL) uses problem sets + explain-lists; essay-driven (English A, Portuguese A, Econ evaluation) uses Q/E/C blocks that are already paragraph skeletons.
3. **The error log is the primary table.** Every wrong answer gets a typed entry with a one-sentence fix; an error closes only after two spaced correct reattempts; the weekly *closed-vs-added* delta is the main health metric.
4. **Per-topic readiness is computed, not felt**: test-ready = last diagnostic >= 85% + all errors closed + explain-list answered cold. Confidence is recorded separately so calibration gaps are visible (maths overconfidence is the documented risk).
5. **Rolling retrieval in three tiers** (daily / every 3-4 days / fortnightly) keeps every subject within 14 days of test-ready; a test announcement only *adds* a past-paper sprint, it never starts learning from zero.
6. **One weekly mixed problem set per rotating science/math subject**, ~50% current and ~50% older units, shuffled (Rohrer, d = 0.83).
7. **Fortnightly 20-30 minute mini-diagnostic per subject**, timed and markscheme-marked, is the cadence the evidence favours (bi-weekly beat daily); its scores update readiness.
8. **A 14-day sprint template** auto-generated on announcement: days 1-4 red topics, 5-10 two full past papers, 11-13 error reattempts + explain-list, 14 light review and early sleep.
9. **IA/EE are scheduled like subjects from DP1**, with internal milestones ahead of school dates and a criterion self-score per draft; EE work starts in the DP1-DP2 summer; every draft gets teacher feedback at the *skeleton* stage.
10. **Fixed daily shape**: 16:35-18:30 two 50-min sprints on the hardest new work; dinner break; 20:00-21:00 reading/writing/IA; 21:00-21:30 retrieval; hard stop by 22:00, sleep by 23:00. If it doesn't fit, the plan is wrong, not the student.
11. **Weekly hours target ~10-15 outside class in ordinary weeks, 15-25 in sprint weeks**, tracked as sprints-completed, not minutes sat.
12. **Consistency is the predicted-grade lever**: the tracker keeps every class test, timed essay and IA draft score with teacher feedback verbatim and the action taken, because teachers predict from stable trends across many small data points and read reliability signals at the margin. Treat DP1 June exams and the first DP2 term as the decisive evidence window for early applications.
13. **No streaks anywhere.** All habit metrics are counts, rates and 7-day rolling windows (Lally: one miss is harmless; Polivy & Herman: a reset framing is what causes quitting).
14. **The nightly report is process-first**: what the plan got wrong, one next action, tomorrow's first sprint written out, process-praise language, a self-compassion prompt on missed days, and a "shutdown complete" mark.
15. **Identity metric, not outcome metric, on the dashboard**: "inbox processed X/7 days" and "errors closed this week" are the visible proof of "most organized student"; the 45 and Stanford stay as wall anchors, rehearsed as process, not as score.
